# Assessment Design

Design evidence of learning that measures the intended performance, not familiarity with an incidental tool or format.

## Procedure

If the user supplies an existing assessment, rubric, or task and asks for review or improvement, do not redesign it from scratch. Evaluate it against the Validation checklist, report each unmet item with specific evidence, and propose targeted revisions.

For new designs, follow all seven steps when the user explicitly asks for a full/complete/end-to-end assessment plan, or asks for both formative and summative components plus feedback. For all other requests (e.g., a single rubric, a task, a feedback plan), produce only the parts of the default deliverable that match the request.

1. Read the objectives, learner profile, delivery constraints, and decisions the assessment will inform.
   When this skill is used independently and objectives, learner profile, or delivery constraints have not already been established in the conversation, state your assumptions in a brief "Assumptions" section and proceed; ask a clarifying question only if the objective itself cannot be reasonably inferred.
2. Define acceptable evidence for each objective using observable criteria and performance standards.
3. Choose formative checks for feedback and summative tasks for achievement decisions where required.
4. Design authentic tasks with realistic conditions, clear instructions, and accessible materials.
5. Create a rubric, sample responses, or an answer key. Specify what counts as success and how partial performance is interpreted.
6. Plan timely, specific feedback and opportunities for revision where appropriate.
7. When time and access permit, recommend a brief pilot: who tests, what they do, and what signals indicate ambiguous criteria or irrelevant barriers. Use any pilot results to revise criteria. If a pilot is not feasible before delivery, suggest a rapid review by a colleague or subject-matter expert and flag criteria that remain unvalidated; piloting is a recommendation, not a prerequisite.

## Assessment Options

- Retrieval questions, exit tickets, demonstrations, and short explanations for formative checks.
- Projects, case analyses, simulations, performances, or practical tasks for application and transfer.
- Portfolios for evidence of growth: require artifact selection, context, reflection, and revision history.
- Peer review for judgment and feedback skills: train reviewers, provide criteria and templates, rotate partners, and include reflection.
- Self-assessment for metacognition: ask learners to predict performance, evaluate evidence, and plan improvements.

Use the cognitive, affective, or psychomotor domains that match the objectives. Do not infer values or attitudes from a single response, or competence from completion alone.

## Templates

Default deliverable for general assessment-design requests and full plans: an alignment table, one rubric (or answer key) for each summative task, and a feedback plan. For narrower requests, include only the components that match the request; for reviews or improvements, use the review procedure above. Add other sections (e.g., pilot plan, portfolio or peer-review design) only when required by the applicable Procedure steps, when the user asks for them, or when they are clearly required by the stated objectives.

Use the formats below as adaptable starting points, not fixed requirements. Replace the illustrative content with details that fit the objectives and assessment; include only formats relevant to the request.

Alignment table: map each objective to evidence of learning, success criteria, feedback, and timing so the assessment can be checked for alignment.

| Objective | Evidence/task | Success criteria | Feedback | Timing |
|-----------|---------------|------------------|----------|--------|
| Evaluate a source's credibility | Compare two sources and justify a choice | Applies relevance, authority, and corroboration criteria with supporting evidence | Criterion-based comments and revision | During practice and final task |

Rubric structure: describe performance levels for each relevant criterion. Tailor the criteria and level descriptions to the task; the example row is illustrative, not a required criterion.

| Criterion | Meets standard | Developing | Not yet demonstrated |
|-----------|----------------|------------|----------------------|
| Justification | Uses relevant evidence and explains its connection to the decision | Gives evidence but connection is incomplete | Gives an unsupported decision |

Feedback prompt: use these questions to give criterion-based feedback that names what is working, what needs revision, and a concrete next step. Adapt the wording to the task and learner.

- Which criterion does this work meet most clearly, and what evidence shows it?
- Which criterion is not yet met, and what specifically is missing?
- What is one concrete change to make before the next submission?

## Validation

- Every assessed criterion maps to an objective.
- Learners know the conditions, criteria, and available support in advance.
- Formative assessment occurs during learning, not only after completion.
- Scoring is consistent across reviewers and alternative response formats.
- Accessibility adjustments preserve the intended performance.
- Transfer tasks require meaningful application in a new context.
