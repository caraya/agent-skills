# Delivery — Classroom

This skill adapts instructional design principles for synchronous, instructor-led learning across subjects. It covers modeling disciplinary thinking and practice, managing group dynamics, real-time pacing, immediate feedback loops, and engagement in shared learning spaces (physical or virtual).

## Features

- **Modeling**: Structured examples that make disciplinary thinking visible, such as a demonstration, close reading, source analysis, or worked interpretation
- **Real-Time Pacing**: Matching pace to group comprehension, reading the room, adjusting on the fly
- **Immediate Feedback**: Live Q&A, quick checks, pair feedback, and correction in the moment
- **Group Dynamics**: Managing participation equity, preventing dominance, building psychological safety
- **Engagement Strategies**: Polling, think-pair-share, live participation, discussion facilitation
- **Structured Breaks**: Strategic pauses protecting attention and enabling cognitive consolidation

## When To Use

- Designing instructor-led sessions or courses
- Preparing synchronous online classes (Zoom, Teams, etc.)

## Inputs

The user should provide the following inputs

- **Outcomes**: Desired learning objectives and skills to be acquired
- **Audience**: Learner characteristics, prior knowledge, and experience level
- **Duration**: Total number of class sessions and how long each session will be
- **Modality**: In-person, virtual (online), or hybrid (in-room and remote learners simultaneously). For hybrid, design every interaction so remote and in-room learners can participate equivalently (e.g., shared polling tool, chat monitor, mixed breakout pairs).
- **Class Size**: Number of participants
- **Constraints**: Any limitations such as technology, room setup, or accessibility requirements

Required inputs: Outcomes, Audience, Duration (number and length of sessions), Modality, Class Size. Optional: Constraints (assume none if not stated). If any required input is missing, stop and ask for it before designing the session.

## Outputs

- **Agenda**: A detailed plan of the session including timing for each segment
- **Activities**: Interactive exercises, demonstrations, and practice opportunities
- **Checks**: Methods for assessing understanding and engagement throughout the session
- **Accessibility Options**: Adjustments to accommodate diverse learner needs
- **Evaluation**: Criteria and methods for assessing the overall effectiveness of the session for both formative and summative evaluations
- For multi-session courses, provide a course-level outline (objectives per session, spaced recap links) followed by a detailed agenda for each session.

## Guidelines

### Session Structure (General)
- [ ] Clear learning objectives stated at the start
- [ ] Do not allow any uninterrupted instructor-input segment to exceed 20 minutes (aim for 15); follow each with an interaction or pause.
- [ ] Choose a mix of instructor input, discussion, analysis, guided practice, or independent work that fits the learning objectives
- [ ] Closing recap with next steps and resources
- [ ] Time explicitly reserved for Q&A

### Modeling a Process or Way of Thinking
- [ ] Make the reasoning visible through a relevant example, such as a demonstration, close reading, source analysis, or interpretation of evidence
- [ ] Show how to respond to ambiguity, a plausible mistake, or competing interpretations
- [ ] Explain the rationale behind key steps or judgments (think-aloud protocol)
- [ ] Provide clear checkpoints so learners can follow or rejoin
- [ ] Prepare examples and materials in advance, with room for questions
- [ ] Make visual materials readable at presentation distance and describe important changes aloud
- [ ] Pause frequently for questions; silence is OK
- [ ] If institutional policy, local resources, delivery method, and learner permission allow recording, protect learner privacy when recording sessions for replay

### Real-Time Pacing

**Signs you're going too fast**:
- Confused faces, lack of note-taking, few questions
- Learners asking "wait, what was that?"
- Multiple hands raised with similar clarification questions

**Signs you're going too slow**:
- Fidgeting, side conversations, checking phones
- Early finishers waiting passively
- Questions veering off-topic (sign of attention drift)

**Pacing strategies**:
- Poll the room: "How many followed that? Show with fingers 1-5."
- Graduated complexity: begin with a more accessible example or question, then move toward greater complexity or ambiguity
- Offer parallel ways to engage: "If you're ready, compare these interpretations; otherwise, start by identifying the evidence"
- Build in 5-minute buffer before hard deadline for recap/catch-up

### Immediate Feedback Loops

- [ ] Frequent quick checks: polls, thumbs up/down, raised hands
- [ ] Examine mistakes or competing interpretations constructively: "Let's look at why this approach didn't work" or "What evidence supports each reading?"
- [ ] Pair-and-share: "Turn to your neighbor and explain your interpretation or reasoning"
- [ ] Open Q&A with no penalty: "Any question? It probably helps 3 others"
- [ ] Live chat moderation (for virtual): monitor for confusion, answer publicly
- [ ] Post-activity: "What was confusing? Raise your hand or chat"

### Group Dynamics & Inclusion

**Preventing dominance**:
- [ ] Intentional hand-raising process: wait, give thinkers time, go to non-dominant hands first
- [ ] Structured turn-taking: "We'll hear from 3 people, then move on"
- [ ] Breakout rooms: pair or small-group discussion before whole-group sharing
- [ ] Think-pair-share: solo reflection → pair discussion → voluntary share. Learners can prepare and discuss their response with a partner before choosing whether to speak to the whole group. As an inclusion safeguard, prefer this lower-pressure starting point for new groups or groups where learners may fear embarrassment or negative judgment; this is not a claim that it builds voluntary participation more effectively than cold-calling.
- [ ] Written responses: poll or chat allows introverts to participate without verbal pressure

**Building psychological safety**:
- [ ] Model mistakes early: "I sometimes skip this step too"
- [ ] Normalize productive struggle: "This is legitimately hard. Working through the challenge can help us learn; if you're stuck, let's find the next step together."
- [ ] Use "we" language: "We're learning this together"
- [ ] Respond to wrong answers constructively: "Good thinking; here's where it diverges"
- [ ] No ridicule or rushed corrections; frame errors as valuable insights

**Accessibility & Neurodiversity**:
- [ ] Visual + auditory: slides, demonstrations, whiteboarding, not audio-only
- [ ] Give advance notice before cold-calling or surprise activities; allow think time or a pass
- [ ] Option to engage via chat/written response (not just verbal)
- [ ] Quiet breaks: "Mute video, grab water, no pressure to engage for 5 min"
- [ ] If recording is permitted and supported by the school or department and delivery platform, clarify whether it will be available for asynchronous review
- [ ] Consider access to spoken content: use live captions when available; if using recordings or pre-recorded materials, provide both captions and transcripts

### Engagement Strategies

**Think-Pair-Share Protocol**:
1. **Think**: Individual reflection, annotation, or response
2. **Pair**: Discuss with a partner or breakout partner
3. **Share**: Invite learners to report back to the whole group

Set the time for each phase based on the activity, learning objective, and depth of engagement needed.

**Building voluntary participation**:
- Recommend regular cold-calling to build toward more volunteers only when paired with all accessibility safeguards: advance notice, think time, and an option to pass. For new groups or groups with low psychological safety, prefer think-pair-share with voluntary sharing instead. In a study of 16 college course sections, voluntary answering rates were higher in classes with frequent cold-calling than in classes with little cold-calling (77% vs. 55%), and rose from 68% to 86% over the term in high-cold-calling classes. [[Study](https://uminntilt.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/impact-of-cold-calling-on-student-voluntary-participation.pdf)]

**Live Polling**:
- Pose a question and invite responses by hand, chat, or polling platform
- Share response patterns and discuss differing interpretations or misconceptions
- Use to diagnose where the group is struggling
- "Let's compare these responses. What evidence or reasoning supports each one?"

**Paired Analysis, Discussion, and Practice**:
- Choose a pair task that fits the objective: interpret a passage, compare sources, discuss evidence for a claim, give feedback on an argument, or practice a procedure
- Partners take turns sharing their reasoning and responding with questions or feedback relevant to the task
- Instructor circulates, asks questions, and supports pairs
- Gives learners a lower-pressure setting to develop and test ideas before sharing with the whole group

**Whiteboarding & Sketching**:
- Visual representation of abstract concepts with equivalent text explanations
- Plan before performing: concept map, process diagram, outline, or sketch
- Reduce cognitive load by externalizing thinking

### Breaks & Consolidation

- [ ] 10-minute break every 50-60 minutes (not just at meals)
  - A study of 72 university students found that a 5-minute unstructured rest after a demanding mental-math task was followed by higher reported directed attention and better problem-solving than no break. This describes that study's procedure and results; the classroom segment limit is the separate requirement above. [[University of Sydney summary](https://www.sydney.edu.au/news-opinion/news/2023/07/04/5-minute-brain-break-refresh-your-mind-attention-psychology-expert.html); [study](https://doi.org/10.1080/20590776.2023.2225700)]
- [ ] Strategic pause: 2-minute silence after difficult content for processing
- [ ] Recap every 20-30 min: "Here's what we just covered..."
- [ ] Spaced summary: beginning of session covers yesterday's key points
- [ ] Parking lot: "Great question—let's write that down and circle back"

## Lesson Structure

### Gagné's Nine Events — Classroom Application

In a classroom session, the instructor plans and facilitates the nine events, choosing their timing and sequence within the session arc. This describes control over instructional delivery, not over learner responses or learning effects.

| Event | Classroom Implementation |
|---|---|
| **1. Gain attention** | Hook: surprising outcome, realistic problem, provocative question, or demo that breaks expectations |
| **2. Inform learner of objectives** | State them verbally + show on slide: "By the end of this, you'll be able to..." |
| **3. Stimulate recall of prior knowledge** | Quick show-of-hands, poll, or 1-min pair discussion: "What do you already know about X?" |
| **4. Present content** | Introduce relevant material, such as a text, source, case, concept, or demonstration; use focused segments with opportunities for interaction |
| **5. Provide learning guidance** | Model a relevant approach, such as close reading, source analysis, interpretation of evidence, or solving a worked example |
| **6. Elicit performance** | Give learners a task aligned with the objective, such as making an interpretation, supporting a claim with evidence, discussing a case, or practicing a procedure |
| **7. Provide feedback** | Respond to learners' reasoning or work using relevant criteria; discuss productive next steps and alternative approaches |
| **8. Assess performance** | Use a quick check aligned with the objective, such as a brief explanation, evidence-based response, poll, or demonstration of a skill |
| **9. Enhance retention & transfer** | Ask learners to connect the idea or skill to a new text, case, question, context, or application; recap the key takeaway |

### Design Patterns

**Model → Supported Practice or Analysis → Independent Application**
- Instructor models an approach when it supports the objective, using a worked example, close reading, source analysis, or interpretation
- Learners try the approach with prompts, shared discussion, or partial scaffolding
- Learners apply it independently to a new problem, text, source, case, or task
- Choose the form of practice to fit the discipline and objective; timebox phases and announce transitions

**Faded Scaffolding**
- Attempt 1: Instructor models a discipline-relevant approach—for example, close-reading a passage, evaluating a historical source, building an evidence-based argument, or demonstrating a procedure; learners observe, annotate, or discuss the reasoning
- Attempt 2: Instructor provides prompts, guiding questions, or partial structure as learners analyze a new passage or source, develop an interpretation, or practice a skill together
- Attempt 3: Learners independently apply the approach to a new text, question, case, artifact, or task; instructor offers feedback as needed
- Adjust the amount and type of support to the learning objective and learners' prior experience; not every objective requires this exact sequence

**Chunking per Segment**
- Organize related information into meaningful chunks. Miller's estimate of 7 ± 2 concerns immediate-memory span for chunks under specific conditions; it is not a target or fixed limit for the number of new concepts in a 20-minute segment
- Introduce unfamiliar terminology, methods, or tool operation in a way that supports—not distracts from—the learning objective
- Name each chunk explicitly so learners can anchor recall

### Activity Checklist (per classroom session)

- [ ] Learning objective stated and visible at the start
- [ ] Prior knowledge activated (poll, warm-up, or discussion)
- [ ] A relevant example or model of disciplinary thinking, when useful for the objective
- [ ] Learner activity or practice with clear criteria appropriate to the task
- [ ] Feedback loop after the activity (whole-group debrief, discussion, or pair share)
- [ ] Closing transfer prompt or reflection question
- [ ] Recap of key mental model at session end

## Session Template: 90-Minute Interactive Class

**0-5 min**: Intro
- Welcome, housekeeping, objectives, what success looks like

**5-10 min**: Warm-up (connect to prior knowledge)
- Think-pair-share: "What do you already know or notice about this topic?"
- Poll or brief written response: surface prior experience, an initial interpretation, or a question

**10-40 min**: Introduce material and explore it together
- Instructor introduces a text, source, case, concept, or procedure, modeling relevant thinking as appropriate (10 min)
- Learners analyze, discuss, interpret, or practice with instructor support (16 min)
- Quick check: poll, pair discussion, or brief written response (2 min)
- Debrief key reasoning, questions, or findings (2 min)

**40-50 min**: Break

**50-80 min**: Apply and extend learning
- Introduce a new or more complex text, source, case, question, or task (10 min)
- Learners apply the learning independently or collaboratively; instructor circulates (10 min)
- Pair share: "Compare your interpretation, evidence, or approach" (5 min)
- Discuss questions, differences, and common challenges (5 min)

**80-90 min**: Wrap-up
- Recap key points (3 min)
- Q&A + parking lot review (4 min)
- Next steps, resources, where to practice (2 min)
- Exit ticket: anonymous feedback or reflection (1 min)

## Recording & Asynchronous Access

**If recording**:
- [ ] Edited, not raw (remove dead time, include captions)
- [ ] Chapters/timestamps marking key segments
- [ ] Show visual materials clearly without captions obscuring essential details
- [ ] Transcript + searchable notes
- [ ] Linked to asynchronous follow-up activities (not just passive video)

**Hybrid approach**:
- Live session focused on interaction, Q&A, relationship-building
- Recording available for those who miss or need replay
- Asynchronous pre-work or post-work activities (self-paced practice)

## Facilitator Skills

### Think-Aloud Protocol
When demonstrating, narrate your mental process:
- "What information do we have?"
- "Which criteria should guide this decision?"
- "That interpretation differs from the others; what evidence or assumptions might explain the difference?"
- "How does this example connect to the broader idea or question?"

### Reading the Room
- **Body language**: Furrowed brows, fidgeting, or note-taking patterns may prompt a check-in; they do not establish comprehension
- **Question cadence**: A change in the number of questions may be a cue to check in, not proof of confusion or engagement
- **Silence patterns**: Allow time for reflection, then invite responses or questions without assuming what silence means
- **Breakout room debrief**: "What was hard? What did you figure out?"

### Redirecting Off-Topic Questions
- "Great question—it's related but beyond today's scope. Let's park that [writes it down]"
- "Can we take that discussion offline so we stay on track?"
- "That's a common edge case; we'll address it after the next demo"

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Impact | Mitigation |
|---------|--------|-----------|
| Showing only a finished result | Learners miss the thinking process | Demonstrate meaningful steps and narrate decisions |
| No breaks during the session, or breaks only at the end of the day | Learners may become fatigued and have difficulty sustaining attention | Schedule breaks during the session, following the guidance in Breaks & Consolidation |
| Dominance by few vocal learners | Quiet learners disengage | Structured turn-taking, think-pair-share, chat option |
| Keeping perfect pace | Some learners lost, others bored | Embrace flexibility; read the room, adjust |
| Recording without transcripts | Inaccessible to deaf/HoH learners | Provide captions and transcript |
| Spending 30 min on one example, question, or discussion | Momentum lost, others frustrated | Set time limits; "Let's move on; I'll follow up on this afterward" |
| Assuming prior knowledge | Jargon unexplained, new learners confused | Explicitly surface assumptions; ask clarifying questions |
| All instruction, no collaboration | Passive, low engagement | Mix 1:1 or pair interaction throughout |
